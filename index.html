Overview

In this homework we built a basic rasterizer that can draw SVG files. We implemented triangle rasterization, supersampling for antialiasing, 2D transforms, color interpolation with barycentric coordinates, and texture mapping with pixel sampling and mipmapping. The biggest takeaway for us was that most differentiating artifacts (jaggies, thin triangles breaking up, moiré in textures) come from aliasing, and most of the assignment was different ways to deal with it.

Task 1: Drawing Single-Color Triangles

To rasterize a triangle, we first find its bounding box (min/max of the vertex x and y, clamped to the screen). Then for every pixel in the box we check whether the pixel center \((x + 0.5, y + 0.5)\) is inside the triangle using the three edge functions (line tests). If the point is on the same side of all three edges, it's inside, and we fill it with the triangle's color. We divide by the triangle's signed area so it works for both clockwise and counterclockwise vertex orders, and we count points exactly on an edge as inside.

This is no worse than checking every sample in the bounding box because that's exactly what it does: each pixel in the bounding box gets checked once with a constant amount of work, and nothing outside the box is checked.

Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

For supersampling with sample rate \(N\), we take an \(\sqrt{N} \times \sqrt{N}\) grid of samples inside each pixel instead of just the center. We changed the sample_buffer to hold width * height * sample_rate colors, so every pixel has its own set of sub-samples. rasterize_triangle now runs the inside test on each sub-sample, fill_pixel (used for lines and points) sets all sub-samples of a pixel to the same color, and resolve_to_framebuffer averages each pixel's sub-samples to get the final color. We also had to fix the buffer resizing in set_sample_rate and set_framebuffer_target to include the sample rate.

Supersampling is useful because edge pixels get a color based on how much of the pixel the triangle actually covers, instead of all-or-nothing. That smooths out jaggies and keeps thin triangles from disappearing.

At sample rate 1, the skinny magenta triangle's tip breaks into disconnected pixels because the triangle is thinner than a pixel and misses most pixel centers. At rate 4 more of it shows up as lighter pink partial coverage, and at rate 16 the tip is continuous and fades out smoothly.

Task 3: Transforms

Our cubeman is pink because he's a ballerina. He has an arm and leg both sticking out to show his pirouette. Each arm and leg is a nested group, so we rotate the upper arm around the shoulder and then rotate the forearm inside that group, which keeps the forearm attached at the elbow even though both pieces are rotated. The swept-out leg is a single rotation applied to the whole leg group around the hip, so both pieces of the leg move together. The head is a square rotated 45°.

Task 4: Barycentric coordinates

Barycentric coordinates \((\alpha, \beta, \gamma)\) describe a point in a triangle as a weighted mix of the three vertices, where the weights add up to 1. Each weight is how close the point is to that vertex: it's 1 at the vertex and 0 on the opposite edge. We can use the same weights to blend anything stored at the vertices, like colors: \(C = \alpha C_0 + \beta C_1 + \gamma C_2\).

Task 5: "Pixel sampling" for texture mapping

Pixel sampling is how we pick a texture color for a screen sample. We use the barycentric coordinates to interpolate the vertices' \((u, v)\) coordinates, which gives a point in the texture, and then look up the color there.

  • Nearest: use the single texel closest to \((u, v)\). It's fast but can look blocky or broken up.
  • Bilinear: take the 4 texels around \((u, v)\) and blend them based on how close the point is to each one. It's smoother but takes 4 lookups instead of 1.

We used the Berkeley seal texture, with the inspector over the white "BERKELEY" lettering on the blue band. The seal is shrunk on screen, so the letters are only a few pixels wide. With nearest at 1 sample per pixel, the letters are harsh and broken up into scattered white pixels, since each pixel either lands on a white texel or misses it. Bilinear at 1 sample per pixel gives smoother, more connected strokes with in-between blue-white shades, because it blends the 4 nearest texels. At 16 samples per pixel both look smooth and almost the same, since averaging 16 lookups per pixel already smooths things out. The two methods differ the most when the texture has thin, high-contrast details (like white text on dark blue) and the texture is stretched or shrunk a lot relative to the screen.
